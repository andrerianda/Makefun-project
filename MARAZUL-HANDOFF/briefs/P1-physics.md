## Conventions (the same ones CONVENTIONS.md states)
1 unit = 1 metre; +Y up; -Z is north, +X is east; sea level is y = 0; angles in radians; seconds; kilograms. Modern ES modules, JSDoc on exports, no frameworks. Each file has one clear job and is named for it, and its first declaration is its main export with a descriptive name. Files of a few hundred lines rather than giants. Deterministic: a fixed time step (the game steps physics at 60 Hz, with substeps where you need them), no Math.random and no Date, no per-step allocation in hot paths (typed arrays, pooled scratch objects).

## The world you simulate
A city on a height field: the ground is given to you by a provider object with a function for the height at x, z, one for the normal, and one for the surface type (asphalt, concrete, sand, grass, dirt, wood, metal, water), plus a wetness factor that lowers grip. The sea is at y = 0 with a wave height function passed in. No road passes over another, so the ground is single valued; bridges are part of the height field. On top of the ground stand static colliders that the world streams in and out by sector: buildings as oriented boxes or convex prisms (yaw rotation and a vertical extent), walls and fences, vertical cylinders (poles, trunks), low curbs, ramps and wedges (stunt jumps, loading docks, stairs as ramps), and trigger volumes that report enter and exit. Moving things: vehicles (up to about sixty active at once, most of them traffic), characters (the player, and up to a few hundred pedestrians and police, most of them cheap), boats, a helicopter or two, knockable street props, thrown objects, debris.

## What to build
1. The collision world: add and remove static colliders and triggers by handle, a spatial hash broadphase, material and surface tags and a user reference on each collider. Queries: ray cast (first hit with point, normal, distance, collider, surface; with layer masks and an ignore list), sphere cast, capsule overlap and sweep, line of sight, the ground under a point; all fast enough to call hundreds of times a step.
2. Character movement: a kinematic capsule controller that collides and slides against statics, vehicles and other characters, climbs curbs and steps up to about 0.35 m, respects a slope limit, handles gravity, jumping, falling and landing, pushes out of penetration, snaps to ground on slopes, reports wading and swimming by water depth, and can be knocked down by an impulse (it reports the event; animation is someone else's). Also a cheap mode for crowds: ground following and simple separation with no sweeps.
3. Vehicle dynamics that feel like an arcade driving game with weight: a rigid body (position, orientation quaternion, linear and angular velocity) on four wheels with ray cast suspension (spring, damper, travel, anti-roll), a tyre model with slip-dependent longitudinal and lateral grip under a traction circle, surface and wetness grip, a handbrake that lets the rear step out and hold a drift, an engine torque curve with an automatic gearbox that exposes engine speed and gear for the audio, braking and reverse, speed-sensitive steering, drag, rolling resistance and a little downforce, a rollover that can happen and an assist that rights the car, stable resting when parked (no creep or jitter, bodies sleep), jumps and landings off ramps. Two-wheelers with a lean model on the same core. Hull collisions (one or two oriented boxes per vehicle) against statics, other vehicles and characters by impulses with restitution and friction, with no tunnelling at 60 m/s, returning impact events (the pair, point, normal, closing speed, energy) for damage, sound and crime detection. A function that derives all tuning from catalog numbers the content team supplies per vehicle (mass, length, width, height, wheelbase, top speed, 0 to 100 km/h time, braking, grip, drift tendency, drive wheels), so every vehicle drives distinctly and reaches its stated top speed. A cheap traffic mode: a vehicle that follows a lane path kinematically (wheels still follow the ground) and switches to full dynamics when hit or taken by the player, and back to sleep when at rest.
4. Boats: buoyancy on the wave surface at several hull points, thrust, rudder, drag, planing at speed, beaching on sand. A helicopter: collective and cyclic style controls reduced to keyboard inputs (lift, pitch, roll, yaw), ground effect, a stable hover, and a helper that flies it toward a target point for police pursuit.
5. Knockable props: light street objects (cones, bins, signs, hydrants, lamp posts, fence panels, crates) are static until hit above a threshold, then become simple rigid bodies (boxes, cylinders) that tumble, bounce, come to rest and sleep; the vehicle that hits them loses a little speed.
6. Projectiles and explosions: a thrown object with gravity, bounce and a fuse; an explosion query that applies impulses to everything in range with falloff and line of sight and returns what it touched and how hard.
7. One world object that owns all of this, steps it at a fixed rate, and exposes an event queue the game drains each step (impacts, trigger enters and exits, knockdowns, props broken, landings, water entries). Bodies carry a user reference so the game can map them to entities. An optional separate module, the only one allowed to import three, that draws colliders, wheels, contacts and rays as lines for debugging.

## Quality bar and tests
Write Node tests under tools/tests/physics/ (run with `node --test`) that prove at least: ray casts and sweeps are correct against known shapes; the capsule slides along walls, climbs a curb, stops at a steep slope and never falls through the ground; for several tunings (a compact, a sports car, a truck, a motorcycle) the vehicle reaches its stated top speed within five percent, stops from 100 km/h in a believable distance, holds a steady circle, drifts with the handbrake and recovers, stays put when parked on a 10 percent slope, lands a jump from a ramp without exploding, and does not pass through a wall at 60 m/s; two cars colliding exchange momentum sensibly; a hit lamp post breaks off and comes to rest; the boat floats and turns; the helicopter holds a hover and reaches a target; the simulation is deterministic (the same inputs give the same digest); and a benchmark of sixty vehicles and three hundred cheap characters on a field of two thousand static colliders steps in well under 4 ms on this machine (print the numbers). Then, when the shared tools exist, write tools/preview/physics.html: a three.js debug view of a scripted test drive (a slalom, a handbrake turn, a jump, a crash into props and a wall) drawn with simple shapes, capture a few frames and check them by eye. Tune the handling until a scripted lap looks and measures right: quick to turn in, stable at speed, satisfying slides, weighty crashes.
